# Of the Awakening of the Nine 

In the days before memory, before the counting of years, and before the
naming of stars and seas, there was Elonia. Yet Elonia was not then as
it is now, nor was it empty.

The mountains had not yet found their roots, and the rivers wandered
without course. The heavens lay in darkness, and no song had ever risen
beneath them. There was neither kingdom nor creature that could speak
its own name, and the world waited.

The oldest myths agree on this truth: the world waited. Whether it
waited by design, by chance, or through some purpose hidden beyond
understanding, no wisdom of god or mortal has ever answered. Yet in
every ancient account - from the earliest temple inscriptions to the
final writings preserved before the Silence - the same reality remains.
The world was not made by the Nine. The Nine came to it.

How they came is not known. The oldest priests spoke of the Sea Beyond,
teaching that before the shaping of the world, there existed a vastness
without shore or boundary wherein all things slumbered unmade. They
called it a sea only because no other word could contain it; for it was
not water, nor darkness, nor void. It was possibility.

From that possibility, the Nine emerged - not together, not in glory,
and not with knowledge of themselves, for it is said that even the gods
were once lost.

The first to awaken was Orinth. Finding himself beneath a sky in which
neither sun nor moon yet shone, the Sage looked upon the world and
perceived that it lacked names. Therefore, he named the stone, he named
the wind, and he named the sea. In the naming of things, he began to
understand them, and thus knowledge was born. Long he wandered alone,
and many ages passed before another awakening came.

Then Selyra opened her eyes in the darkness. The tales say she gazed
upward and dreamt of lights where none yet existed. In her vision, she
beheld silver fires adrift in the endless black, and because she dreamt
them, they came to be. Whether this is truth or allegory none can say,
yet even now, those who study the heavens note that the stars seem to
move according to patterns seen first in dreams.

Orinth and Selyra found one another upon the high places of the world
and walked together throughout uncounted years. Together, they searched
the lands and questioned all that they found. Yet whenever they asked
from where they had come, no answer was given, and whenever they sought
the edge of the world, the horizon fled before them.

Thereafter, the remaining Seven awoke. Aurelion rose amidst storm and
fire. Thaeron awoke upon a battlefield that did not yet exist, as though
war itself had awaited him. Elyndra stirred beneath branches older than
mountains, while Kaelis opened his eyes at the world's furthest horizon
and immediately began walking toward another.

Varek came in silence. No myth speaks clearly of his awakening, and many
ancient texts refuse to describe it at all. Before Varek, there was
neither ending nor departure: creatures lived and never passed, forests
grew and never withered, and memories accumulated without fading. Then
Varek entered the world, and the first leaf fell. Thus came mortality
into creation. Many mortals feared him thereafter, but among the Nine,
none doubted his necessity - for even life requires endings.

Iskara awoke as the first creatures gathered together rather than apart.
She taught no law and built no kingdom, yet by her presence, loyalty was
born, along with affection and the strange willingness of one living
thing to suffer for another.

Last of all came Myrraen. The oldest songs tell that the Eight wandered
together for many ages before they found her standing beside a river,
watching in silence. Aurelion demanded to know who she was, Orinth asked
what she had learned, and Selyra asked what she had seen. To each, she
gave the same answer: "I have seen pain."

These words appear in nearly every surviving version of the tale.
Whether they were truly spoken has long since been lost, yet all
histories agree that from the first day of her awakening, Myrraen looked
upon the world differently than the others. Where Aurelion saw order,
she saw suffering; where Kaelis saw freedom, she saw loneliness; where
Thaeron saw strength, she saw sacrifice; and where the others saw
creation, she saw responsibility. For this reason, many scholars later
argued that the Abandonment began not in the final years of the Age of
Revelation, but in the very hour Myrraen opened her eyes. For all great
tragedies begin as virtues, and no virtue shaped history more completely
than mercy.

Then the Nine gathered together and beheld Elonia. Perceiving that the
world was unfinished, they set their hands to its shaping. The mountains
rose higher, the rivers found their courses, forests spread across the
lands, and the creatures of the world multiplied. Beneath the guidance
of the Nine, there began the First Age, which the ancient chronicles
call the Age of Dawn.

Yet even as the gods labored, another mystery unfolded beyond their
sight. In distant valleys and forgotten plains, life was changing. New
beings walked beneath the stars - small, fragile, short-lived, and
curious. The Nine had made no such creatures, nor had they foreseen
them. Indeed, the oldest divine texts suggest something unsettling: the
gods discovered humanity at almost the same moment they discovered one
another. For the first time since their awakening, the Celestine
encountered something they could neither explain nor predict.

Thus began the long history of gods and mortals - he love that would
bind them together for ages beyond counting, and the road that would one
day lead, though none knew it, to the Silence.

# Of the Coming of Men and the Wonder of the Nine 

Now when the world was young and the labours of the Nine were not yet
complete, there came into being a thing unforeseen. This matter is
counted among the greatest mysteries in all the histories of Elonia, for
neither the songs of the priests nor the memories of the gods themselves
provide any certain answer.

How came Men into the world? None know. The oldest mortals claimed they
had always existed, while the oldest gods claimed they had not. Between
these two certainties lies a contradiction that has endured through
every age.

For the Nine had shaped mountains and forests. They had tended rivers
and guided the flight of stars. They understood the creatures of the
earth, for many they had nurtured and some they had fashioned according
to their own designs. Yet of Men, they possessed no such knowledge.

When first the Celestine encountered them, they found no signs of divine
workmanship. No god claimed them, no god recognized them, and no god
understood them. This astonished the Nine more deeply than any wonder
they had yet beheld.

For Men were small, weak in body, and short in years. They built no
monuments and commanded no magic; their earliest settlements consisted
of little more than timber shelters clustered around fires. Many other
peoples then dwelling in Elonia seemed greater - the giants raised
cities of stone before Men had learned masonry, the dragon-lords crossed
oceans before Men had mastered sailing, and even the elder beastfolk
possessed traditions older than the first human kingdoms.

Yet the Nine found themselves returning again and again to these strange
newcomers. For there was about Men something unsettling, something
impossible to measure, something that defied understanding.

The first among the gods to perceive it was Orinth. Long did he dwell
among the earliest tribes, concealing his majesty so that he might
observe them unnoticed. He listened to their stories, watched their
quarrels, and taught them language while receiving language in return.
In doing so, he discovered a thing that delighted him: Men were
unpredictable. The wolf behaved according to its nature, the eagle
according to its nature, and even dragons, for all their pride and
cunning, rarely acted beyond the boundaries of what they were.

But Men changed. One who was craven in youth might become courageous in
old age; one who was kind might become cruel; one who sought power might
abandon it; and one who possessed nothing might shape the destiny of
thousands. Again and again, humanity refused to remain what it had been.
To Orinth, this was a marvel. For the first time since his awakening, he
encountered a mystery that continued producing new questions - and
questions, above all things, were dear to him.

Yet what delighted Orinth troubled others. Selyra watched humanity
through dreams and saw futures multiplying wherever Men gathered. Before
their coming, the world had possessed a certain order. Not peace, for
the world has never known peace, yet order nonetheless. Events followed
patterns: the movements of kingdoms could be foreseen, the growth of
forests predicted, and the rise of civilizations anticipated centuries
before their beginnings.

But wherever Men walked, certainty diminished. The future fractured and
possibilities multiplied. A single mortal decision might alter the
course of nations, a forgotten conversation might lead to wars, an act
of compassion might save a kingdom, and a moment of cruelty might
destroy one. The paths of destiny became tangled beyond prediction, and
Selyra, gazing into the tides of possibility, saw that humanity stood at
the center of every storm. Therefore, she began to fear them - not
because they were evil, but because they were free.

Aurelion reached a different conclusion. When he looked upon Men, he saw
potential. They built settlements where none had stood, and organized
themselves into families, tribes, councils, and kingdoms. They created
laws even before they understood justice, seeking structure
instinctively, as rivers seek the sea. Thus, Aurelion became their
teacher.

It was he who first instructed kings in governance, taught judges to
separate guilt from innocence, and raised the earliest cities to show
humanity how stone might outlast timber. Under his guidance, the first
kingdoms flourished. Roads crossed vast distances, trade united distant
peoples, learning accumulated, and civilization advanced. For a time,
Aurelion believed he had found the answer to humanity's potential: with
proper guidance, they might become the greatest people the world had
ever known.

Yet among all the Nine, none loved humanity more fiercely than Iskara.
While the others debated destiny, possibility, and power, Iskara
concerned herself with simpler things. She sat beside wedding fires,
comforted parents mourning children, listened to songs, and watched
friends risk their lives for one another. The other gods often judged
humanity according to kingdoms and empires, but Iskara judged them
according to individuals. What mattered to her was not what humanity
could accomplish, but what humanity was willing to sacrifice.

Again and again, she witnessed ordinary people choosing love over
self-interest - a mother surrendering her food so a child might eat, a
hunter risking death to rescue a companion, or a ruler choosing peace
despite personal cost. To Iskara, these small acts outweighed a thousand
crimes. Whenever the other gods despaired, she reminded them of such
things, and for many ages, her arguments prevailed.

Myrraen alone remained uncertain. Not because she loved humanity less -
in truth, she may have loved them more than any of the others - but
unlike Iskara, who saw individual acts, Myrraen saw patterns. Where she
walked, people sought her aid: the sick prayed for healing, the poor
prayed for relief, victims prayed for justice, and the desperate prayed
for mercy. And she answered, always, for how could she refuse? If
compassion carries power, what meaning has compassion if that power is
never used?

Thus, she healed the wounded, fed the hungry, shielded the innocent, and
lifted burdens wherever she found them. Her temples multiplied across
the world, songs were written in her honor, and the White Stag became a
symbol recognized in every nation. Throughout those early centuries, no
god was more beloved.

Yet it was then that a shadow entered her thoughts, for she began to
notice something peculiar. The same families returned generation after
generation, the same kingdoms repeated the same mistakes, and the same
cruelties reappeared despite every lesson. Again and again, disaster
struck, and again and again, the gods prevented its consequences.

At first, this troubled her only slightly, but then the years became
centuries, and the centuries became ages. Still, the pattern remained:
the gods healed wounds, but new wounds followed; the gods ended wars,
but new wars emerged; the gods punished tyrants, but others rose in
their place. It seemed to Myrraen that humanity learned little from
suffering because suffering seldom endured long enough to teach.

She spoke of this concern to Varek, for among the Nine, Varek understood
consequences better than any other. It is said that the Keeper of the
Final Door listened for a long while before answering, saying simply:
*"A thing without an ending has no shape."*

Myrraen never forgot those words. At the time, however, she did not yet
understand them, and few did. For the Age of Revelation had only begun.
The friendship between gods and mortals was at its height, the Nine
walked openly among their people, their temples shone with splendor,
miracles were commonplace, and civilizations flourished as never before.

To later generations, it would seem a golden age beyond imagination. And
perhaps it was. Yet hidden within that age were the seeds of every
tragedy that would follow. For the gods had come to love humanity, and
humanity had come to rely upon the gods. Neither yet understood the
danger in that bond, but history had already begun its long journey
toward the Silence.

Book II: The Age of Revelation

#  Of the First Covenant

Long ages passed between the coming of Men and the first great kingdoms
of the world. The earliest generations of humanity lived much as all
creatures had lived before them - hunting beneath forests that seemed
endless, crossing rivers whose names had not yet been spoken, burying
their dead beneath stone cairns, and telling stories around fires while
storms rolled across the horizon.

In those days, the gods were known only through rumor. Travelers
returned with impossible tales: hunters spoke of a wanderer who knew the
secret name of every bird, shepherds claimed a woman clothed in
moonlight appeared in their dreams to warn them of approaching floods,
and entire villages told stories of a beautiful stranger who healed
fevers merely by touching a child's brow. The gods walked among
humanity, but they did not yet reveal themselves openly.

Partly this arose from caution, and partly from curiosity; the Celestine
wished to understand these strange creatures before shaping their
destinies. For many centuries they watched, and the more they watched,
the more fascinated they became. Humanity possessed no singular nature.
A thousand wolves might behave alike, but a thousand men never would.
The same capacity that inspired great kindness also enabled terrible
cruelty, and the same imagination that created poetry invented
deception. Humanity seemed forever balanced between opposing
possibilities. This perplexed the Nine. They had shaped mountains and
seas, yet they could not predict a single human heart.

Thus the centuries passed. Men multiplied, tribes became settlements,
settlements became towns, the first rulers emerged, the first disputes
over territory began, and the first organized wars stained the earth. At
last, the gods recognized that the age of observation had ended - the
age of guidance had begun.

The event later called the First Covenant occurred in the Valley of
Serath, though whether the valley still bears that ancient name is
disputed. At that time, nine human nations had emerged across the known
world. None were large by later standards, yet all were powerful enough
to influence their neighbors. Competition between them had become
increasingly violent, trade routes were collapsing, raids grew common,
and several regions stood upon the brink of prolonged warfare.

According to surviving accounts, the Nine appeared together for the
first time in history. No record fully agrees regarding what happened -
some claim the skies blazed with divine fire, others describe days of
darkness followed by brilliant dawn, and one version insists that every
living creature fell silent for a single hour. What all versions agree
upon is simpler: the gods revealed themselves openly, without disguise,
and without mystery. Humanity learned that it was not alone.

The rulers of the nine nations gathered before the Celestine, and there
the First Covenant was sworn. The precise wording has been lost; indeed,
scholars have spent centuries arguing over whether a single text ever
existed. Yet all surviving fragments preserve the same essential
meaning. The gods promised knowledge, protection, and guidance, while
humanity promised remembrance, devotion, and stewardship of the world
they shared.

Neither side pledged obedience. This detail proved enormously
significant: the Celestine did not claim dominion over humanity, nor did
humanity surrender its freedom. The Covenant established partnership,
not ownership. To later generations, this distinction became sacred; to
historians, it became tragic. For every great conflict that followed
emerged from different interpretations of that promise.

The centuries after the Covenant became known as the Age of Revelation.
Many later peoples imagined it as a perfect era, but it was not. There
remained wars, famines, crimes, and disasters; human suffering did not
vanish. The difference lay in humanity's relationship with the divine,
as for the first time, gods and mortals built civilization together.

Aurelion taught the foundations of law, his scholars codifying systems
of governance that influenced kingdoms for millennia - the concept that
rulers themselves could be subject to written law traces its origins to
his teachings. Orinth transformed isolated collections of knowledge into
the first true libraries; under his influence, language became
standardized, mathematics flourished, astronomy advanced, and entire
disciplines emerged from questions he encouraged humanity to ask.
Elyndra taught sustainable agriculture and stewardship of the natural
world, so that regions touched by her guidance became renowned for
fertility and abundance, with farmers regarding her almost as a
companion.

Thaeron instructed warriors not merely in combat, but in discipline, the
earliest knightly traditions finding their origin in his belief that
strength was meaningless unless exercised in service to others. Iskara
reshaped society in subtler ways, her followers formalizing marriage
traditions, systems of adoption, festivals of remembrance, and communal
celebrations that survived long after her departure. Kaelis inspired
explorers to cross oceans and mountain ranges previously considered
impassable, with many of the age's greatest discoveries arising because
travelers sought paths he hinted might exist. Selyra founded
observatories and dream sanctuaries where prophets attempted to
understand the patterns hidden within existence, while Varek established
great Houses of Remembrance where the names of the dead were preserved
for future generations. And everywhere Myrraen traveled, hospitals,
sanctuaries, and houses of refuge followed.

The ancient world bloomed. Throughout Elonia, immense cities arose,
though none were the personal domains of gods. The Celestine consciously
rejected mortal worship directed toward the accumulation of power,
choosing instead to cultivate civilizations capable of governing
themselves. This principle would later become central to Myrraen's
argument during the final debates before the Abandonment: the gods had
never intended humanity to become servants, but heirs.

Ancient cities reflected this belief. The city of Solcaris, favored by
Aurelion, became famous for its courts and academies of law. Lunareth,
beloved of Selyra, housed great celestial observatories whose towers
seemed to touch the stars. Ilyanor, where Orinth spent much of his time,
became the greatest center of learning ever known, with entire
philosophies emerging from its libraries as thousands traveled there
seeking wisdom.

Among all the cities of the ancient age, none became more beloved than
Vale Myrr. Situated between forests and rivers, Vale Myrr grew around a
sanctuary founded by Myrraen herself. It possessed no walls, which
shocked foreign visitors. When neighboring rulers questioned the wisdom
of this decision, Myrraen is said to have replied, *"Those who seek
shelter should never meet a gate."* For centuries, no army ever marched
against it - such was the reputation she commanded.

Yet even as civilization flourished, subtle tensions emerged. The gods
saw them, though most mortals did not. Humanity relied increasingly upon
divine intervention: a failed harvest could be corrected, a plague could
be cured, and a city destroyed by earthquake could be rebuilt. Whenever
catastrophe arose, the Celestine lessened its impact.

At first this seemed an unqualified blessing; how could mercy possibly
become harmful? Yet among the Nine, disagreements slowly developed.
Varek noted that mortality gave life meaning because loss gave memory
value. Thaeron argued that courage existed because danger existed.
Kaelis feared a civilization shielded from consequences would forget how
to adapt.

Myrraen listened closely to these concerns - more closely than anyone
realized. For though she remained beloved by humanity and cherished by
her fellow gods, a question had begun to grow within her heart that she
could not silence: *If every wound is healed, what teaches caution? If
every mistake is repaired, what teaches responsibility? If every tragedy
is prevented, what teaches wisdom?*

At first, she dismissed such thoughts, as compassion demanded action,
did it not? But the question returned again and again, decade after
decade, century after century.

The Age of Revelation had become the greatest flowering civilization
would ever know, yet buried within its success lay the seed of its
destruction. The gods could not yet see it, and humanity certainly could
not. Only in later ages would historians look back upon those radiant
centuries and understand that the road to the Silence began not in
hatred, war, or betrayal, but in love.

#  Of the Nine Cities and the Glory of the First Age

The centuries that followed the First Covenant brought forth a
prosperity unlike any the world would ever know again. Later ages
remembered this period imperfectly - poets transformed it into legend,
priests into theology, and kings into propaganda. By the time of the
Silence, many already spoke of those ancient centuries as though they
had been a paradise.

They were mistaken; paradise had never existed. There was hunger,
rivalry, and sorrow; men still buried their dead and feared the future.
Yet it is equally true that something extraordinary occurred during
those years, for the first and only time in history, gods and mortals
labored side by side toward the same future - not a future of conquest
or dominion, but one of improvement. The Nine believed humanity capable
of greatness, and humanity believed the gods wished to help them achieve
it. Between those two convictions, an age of wonders was born. Men would
later call it the Age of Revelation, while the gods called it simply the
Age of Hope.

The greatest achievements of that age were the Nine Cities. Contrary to
later belief, these were not kingdoms ruled directly by the Celestine,
as the gods had little interest in thrones - indeed, several refused
offers of kingship repeatedly. Rather, each city grew around certain
teachings, philosophies, and ambitions embodied by one of the Nine. In
time, they became symbols: living demonstrations of what humanity could
become.

The first among them was Solcaris. Raised upon sunlit plains beside the
Sea of Glass, Solcaris emerged beneath the guidance of Aurelion. Its
white walls could supposedly be seen from many miles away, gleaming like
polished ivory beneath the sun.

The city was renowned not for its beauty, but for its order. Within
Solcaris, laws were written and preserved rather than remembered through
oral tradition; public offices existed independently of individual
rulers; and taxation, citizenship, rights, and responsibilities were
defined with a precision unknown elsewhere in the world. Visitors often
complained that everything in Solcaris seemed governed by rules, but its
defenders considered this the highest praise imaginable.

*"The law,"* Aurelion taught, *"must be stronger than the king who
enforces it."*

In later centuries, countless rulers would quote those words while
violating them entirely, but during the First Age, they represented a
radical idea: power should submit to principle, not the reverse.

Far to the east stood Ilyanor, beloved of Orinth. If Solcaris
represented order, Ilyanor represented curiosity. The city's libraries
contained more books than many kingdoms possessed citizens, scholars
crossed oceans simply to study there, and astronomers mapped the stars
from towers whose upper chambers seemed suspended among the clouds.

The people of Ilyanor developed an unusual reputation among their
neighbors: they asked too many questions. They questioned magic, nature,
history, and the gods themselves - especially the gods. Many cities
considered this dangerous, but Orinth encouraged it. Indeed, it was said
that no question could offend him except the refusal to ask one.

In the marketplaces of Ilyanor, philosophers debated beggars,
mathematicians argued with sailors, and apprentices were taught not
merely to memorize knowledge, but to challenge it. Knowledge, Orinth
believed, was not sacred because it was correct; knowledge was sacred
because it could be improved. This conviction would one day shape the
future more profoundly than anyone realized.

Among all the Nine Cities, none possessed a stranger reputation than
Nythara. Hidden among silver mountains, the city was devoted to Selyra
and the mysteries she governed. Its temples possessed no roofs, its
highest towers were open to the night sky, and pools of dark water stood
throughout the city, perfectly still regardless of weather. Within them,
seers claimed, the future could occasionally be glimpsed.

Many visiting dignitaries found Nythara unsettling. Citizens often
behaved as though listening to voices nobody else could hear - a woman
might alter her travel plans because of a dream, a merchant might refuse
an enormously profitable opportunity because of an omen, or a ruler
might delay a declaration of war because the stars appeared wrong. For
outsiders, such behavior seemed irrational, yet Nythara prospered. Again
and again, its leaders avoided disasters that should have destroyed
them, appearing prepared for events no mortal ought to have foreseen.

When questioned about this, Selyra supposedly offered only a single
answer: *"The future speaks constantly. Most people simply prefer not to
listen."*

Where Nythara pursued mysteries, Vale Myrr pursued mercy. The city stood
within a broad river valley surrounded by forests and fertile farmland.
Unlike every major settlement of the era, it possessed no walls - a
decision that horrified military leaders throughout the world. Even
peaceful settlements built defenses, but Vale Myrr did not.

The city declared itself a sanctuary: anyone fleeing violence,
persecution, war, or poverty could claim refuge there. No army was
maintained, no prisons were constructed, and no citizen was denied
shelter. Aurelion regarded the experiment as naive, and Thaeron
considered it reckless, yet century after century, the city endured.
Travelers came from every corner of the world to witness it, and many
remained.

Some historians later argued that Vale Myrr represented the purest
expression of Myrraen's philosophy. Mercy was not weakness; mercy
required faith - faith that people could become better than they were,
and that kindness, given freely enough, might eventually teach others to
practice it. For many centuries, that faith seemed justified.

Beyond the northern forests stood Harad Veyr, the city most associated
with Thaeron. Popular imagination often depicts it as a fortress, but
this reveals more about later generations than the city itself. Harad
Veyr revered strength, certainly, yet strength was viewed not as a means
of domination, but as a responsibility. Every citizen received martial
training, every soldier studied ethics, and every commander swore
binding oaths concerning the conduct of warfare.

The city's greatest heroes were not conquerors, but protectors - statues
celebrated those who held impossible defensive positions, rescued
civilians, or sacrificed themselves for others. To the people of Harad
Veyr, courage meant enduring fear rather than lacking it, a mindset
Thaeron approved of greatly.

Across countless miles of wilderness stretched settlements dedicated to
Elyndra. Unlike the other gods, she never favored a single great
metropolis. This preference confused later historians until they
recognized a simple truth: Elyndra did not love cities; she loved life.

Under her influence, vast networks of settlements emerged that existed
in harmony with surrounding ecosystems. Forests were cultivated rather
than cleared, rivers protected rather than redirected, and animals
monitored rather than exterminated. These regions became unimaginably
fertile, and many later agricultural methods would trace their origins
directly to Elyndran traditions. Even today, some wilderness preserves
are believed to occupy land first sanctified by the Verdant Queen
herself.

The remaining cities flourished likewise: Kaelis inspired ports from
which explorers vanished into the unknown; Iskara's centers of art and
celebration produced music and literature studied thousands of years
later; and Varek's great necropolises preserved the names of the dead so
faithfully that entire bloodlines can still be traced through surviving
records. Everywhere, the hand of the divine could be seen, and
everywhere, civilization advanced.

To later generations, it appeared that humanity stood upon the threshold
of perfection. The world grew wealthier, safer, more educated, and more
connected. Disease diminished, life expectancy increased, knowledge
spread, and trade expanded. Many genuinely believed history's great
struggles were ending, and the gods themselves occasionally entertained
similar hopes.

This belief, more than any miracle, revealed how little they yet
understood humanity. For the very success of the Age of Revelation
concealed a growing danger: as generations passed, divine intervention
became expected. Drought was no longer a catastrophe because a god would
help; famine was no longer feared because the temples would provide
relief; and the outbreak of disease became a challenge rather than a
crisis because someone would heal it.

Whenever disaster threatened, the Celestine intervened. Because they
intervened, humanity prospered, and because humanity prospered, the
interventions continued. No one intended harm, and no one recognized the
consequences - not at first.

Only Varek seemed troubled. When Myrraen once asked why, he led her to a
garden at the edge of Vale Myrr. There, beneath a tree shedding autumn
leaves, the Keeper of the Final Door picked up a single fallen leaf and
placed it within her hand.

*"Tell me,"* he asked, *"what gives this leaf its beauty?"*

Myrraen did not understand. "The colors," she answered. *"The shape. The
season."*

Varek shook his head. Its beauty, he said, came from the fact that it
would never exist again. A thousand new leaves would grow, but not this
one - not ever. The ending gave it meaning.

Myrraen remembered the conversation long after Varek departed. Years
later she would remember it again, and centuries later she would build
an entire philosophy upon it. For even during the greatest age the world
had ever known, she had begun to realize something disturbing: a life
protected from every consequence might also be protected from every
lesson. And though she did not yet know it, that thought would one day
divide heaven itself.

# Of the Great Concord

There came a time, nearly two thousand years after the First Covenant,
when it seemed that the hopes of gods and mortals had finally been
fulfilled. The kingdoms of the world remained distinct, yet war between
them had become increasingly rare. Ships crossed oceans carrying goods
from distant lands, scholars traveled freely along roads maintained by
dozens of cooperating nations, great libraries exchanged knowledge,
harvests grew larger, trade expanded, and disease declined. Every
generation seemed wealthier than the one before it.

Later historians would name this period the Great Concord. To those
living within it, however, such a title would have appeared unnecessary.
The Great Concord did not feel exceptional; it felt permanent. That was
its danger.

Children were born who had never known famine, and entire regions forgot
what large-scale war looked like. Vast populations grew up assuming that
if disaster ever struck, the gods would intervene. And why would they
not? The gods always had. When drought threatened crops, Elyndra's
priesthood brought relief. When disease emerged, sanctuaries dedicated
to Myrraen healed the afflicted. When conflicts threatened to escalate,
emissaries traveled beneath Aurelion's banners and negotiated peace.

Generation after generation lived beneath divine protection. The
extraordinary became ordinary, miracles became expected, and expectation
slowly transformed gratitude into assumption.

Few noticed the change, and the gods certainly did not at first, for
they too had become accustomed to the arrangement. The Covenant had
endured far longer than anyone had imagined. Civilization flourished,
knowledge spread, and the world seemed to be advancing. If there were
problems, they appeared manageable, temporary, and correctable. The
future seemed bright; for perhaps the only time in history, both gods
and mortals believed they were winning.

Among the Celestine, none delighted in this age more than Iskara. For it
was not kingdoms that convinced her humanity was worthy, but people. She
attended weddings in countless lands, often unrecognized. She listened
to songs written by farmers, and watched children dance in festival
squares. Again and again she witnessed the same thing: love. Not grand
acts or legendary sacrifices, but ordinary affection - the small
kindnesses that held societies together.

Whenever the gods argued about humanity's failings, Iskara could always
produce another story: a soldier carrying an injured enemy from a
battlefield, a daughter caring for an aging parent, or a stranger
feeding someone they would never meet again. To Iskara these mattered
more than any empire, and during the Great Concord they seemed more
common than ever.

Orinth likewise viewed the age with optimism. The universities of
Ilyanor had become wonders known throughout the world, and new
discoveries emerged constantly. Scholars mapped the stars, engineers
devised machines capable of astonishing feats, languages were studied,
compared, and catalogued, and mathematicians proposed theories that
stretched beyond imagination.

Some even began questioning the gods themselves. A few priests regarded
this as dangerous, but Orinth found it delightful. The students who
challenged divine wisdom were often invited to debate him personally.
Many arrived expecting punishment; instead, they received encouragement.
The greatest gift Orinth ever offered humanity was not knowledge, but
permission - permission to question, to disagree, and to imagine a
future different from the past.

Yet there were moments, rare though they seemed at the time, when
shadows appeared. A ruler delayed addressing a growing crisis because
divine aid would surely arrive. A city neglected preparations against
floodwaters because sacred intervention had protected them before. Some
wars were no longer prevented by diplomacy; instead, they were ended by
miracle after they began. The results appeared identical, yet subtle
differences existed: fewer people learned from catastrophe when
catastrophe seldom reached its natural conclusion.

Myrraen observed such moments quietly. At first she dismissed her
concerns as cynicism. After all, surely preventing suffering remained
the correct choice; surely compassion could not be harmful. And yet the
question lingered - not loudly, not urgently, but persistently: *What
becomes of strength when hardship disappears?*

# Of Avarr, Jewel of the World

Among all the civilizations born during the Great Concord, none shone
more brightly than Avarr. Its rise seemed almost inevitable. Situated at
the convergence of major trade routes and blessed with fertile land,
navigable rivers, and extraordinary political stability, Avarr prospered
with remarkable speed.

Yet geography alone could not explain its success. The people of Avarr
embraced every gift of the age. They built universities inspired by
Orinth, courts modeled on Aurelion's teachings, public hospitals
dedicated to Myrraen, vast gardens cultivated according to Elyndra's
principles, observatories honoring Selyra, and military academies
founded upon Thaeron's codes.

Visitors often described Avarr as the closest thing the world possessed
to perfection. Its literacy rates surpassed every rival nation, its laws
were admired across continents, and its wealth seemed inexhaustible. The
emperor of Avarr became known by a title usually reserved for heroes:
the First Steward. For Avarrans insisted their ruler served the people
rather than ruled them.

In those days many believed Avarr represented the future of civilization
itself. Aurelion admired it, Orinth celebrated it, and even Myrraen
regarded it with pride. Humanity had built something extraordinary -
proof that the Covenant had worked, proof that wisdom could triumph, and
proof that civilization could become better. For nearly four centuries
Avarr justified every hope the gods had ever placed in humanity.

That is why what followed proved so devastating. History often imagines
evil arriving from darkness, but Avarr revealed a more frightening
truth: sometimes evil emerges from certainty.

The crisis began with a discovery. In the southern territories,
explorers encountered a people previously unknown to the empire. They
were smaller in number, less technologically advanced, and politically
fragmented. At first contact was peaceful: trade began, knowledge was
exchanged, and relations appeared promising.

Then philosophers within Avarr proposed a theory. The theory claimed
that civilization itself followed stages of development: some societies
were more advanced, while others were less advanced. Such ideas were not
unprecedented; what made them dangerous was what came next.

A growing faction argued that Avarr possessed a moral obligation to
guide lesser peoples. At first this guidance appeared benevolent.
Schools were established, infrastructure was built, and officials were
dispatched. Yet resistance emerged - not everyone wished to become
Avarran.

The response transformed everything. Gradually, the language of
stewardship became the language of superiority, the language of
superiority became the language of necessity, and the language of
necessity became the language of atrocity.

What followed remains one of the darkest chapters in Elonian history.
Entire cultures were systematically dismantled, languages forbidden,
traditions erased, communities displaced, and countless lives destroyed.
And perhaps most horrifying of all, many perpetrators genuinely believed
they were acting for the greater good. Avarr had not descended into
madness; it had reasoned itself there.

The gods intervened. Of course they intervened. The atrocities ended,
the leaders responsible were removed, and the victims were protected. By
every practical measure, justice prevailed.

Yet among the Celestine, something irreparable had changed - especially
within Myrraen. For she had spent centuries believing suffering created
cruelty. Avarr challenged that belief. Avarr had not been desperate, it
had not been starving, and it had not been oppressed. It had possessed
wealth, education, stability, divine guidance, and prosperity beyond
measure. And still it had chosen destruction.

The realization haunted her. For if paradise itself could not prevent
evil, then perhaps the problem ran deeper than anyone had imagined.

# Of Questions Without Answers

The centuries following Avarr were prosperous. Outwardly, little
changed. The cities remained magnificent, trade continued, the gods
still walked among mortals, and the Covenant endured. Yet the confidence
of earlier ages had begun to fade.

For the first time, difficult questions emerged - questions nobody could
answer. Why did humanity continue repeating old mistakes despite
possessing every advantage imaginable? Why did wisdom fail to produce
virtue? Why did prosperity fail to eliminate cruelty? Why did knowledge
fail to prevent corruption?

These debates spread throughout temples, courts, academies, and palaces,
and increasingly they spread among the gods themselves.

Most believed the answers would eventually be found. History had always
moved forward, problems had always been solved, and the Age of
Revelation had survived every challenge before.

Yet future generations would identify this period as a turning point -
not because of any single war, nor because of any catastrophe, but
because doubt had entered paradise. And once doubt enters, certainty
rarely returns unchanged.

# Of the Century of Interventions

Long after the fall of Avarr, the outward splendor of the Age of
Revelation endured. The roads remained busy, the libraries remained
full, the temples remained crowded, and the harvests remained plentiful.
To ordinary people, it appeared that civilization continued much as it
always had.

Yet beneath the surface, subtle changes were taking place. The world
seemed increasingly unable to solve its problems without divine
assistance. No single crisis caused this condition; rather, it emerged
gradually from centuries of success.

When crops failed in earlier ages, communities had been forced to adapt.
They developed new techniques, migrated to more fertile lands, or
endured hardship until better seasons arrived. Now Elyndra's servants
could restore entire harvests. When disease appeared, physicians once
struggled for years to understand its nature. Now sanctuaries dedicated
to Myrraen cured outbreaks before they could spread far. When kingdoms
marched to war, Aurelion's emissaries often imposed settlements before
campaigns had truly begun.

At first this was celebrated, then expected, and finally, assumed.
Generation after generation grew accustomed to living in a world where
consequences seldom reached their natural end.

The gods themselves did not immediately recognize the change. How could
they? Their interventions had saved countless lives, the suffering they
prevented was real, and the gratitude they received was genuine. Yet
wisdom, like a muscle, weakens when never exercised. Without intending
it, the Celestine had begun shielding humanity not merely from disaster,
but from responsibility.

Of all the Nine, Varek perceived the danger first. It was his nature to
notice endings. He saw rulers becoming careless because catastrophe
never lasted, cities neglecting preparation because divine aid always
arrived, and societies forgetting lessons that earlier civilizations had
learned through hardship.

One evening, within the Hall of Memory, he spoke privately to Myrraen.
The conversation would later acquire near-mythical status, though no
record survives in full - only fragments. One fragment claims that Varek
asked her whether she remembered the names of the first kingdoms.
Myrraen answered that she did. Then Varek asked whether those kingdoms
still existed. Myrraen answered that they did not.

To this Varek supposedly replied: *"Then why do you remember them?"*

The goddess did not answer, for she already knew. Meaning arises not
from permanence; meaning arises from fragility. The thought haunted her,
and for the first time in her long existence, Myrraen began wondering
whether mercy itself could produce harm.

# Of the Dream of Selyra

During the eighth century of the Great Concord, a strange event
occurred. One evening Selyra vanished.

Such disappearances were not entirely unusual. The Moonmother had always
been the most elusive among the Celestine, often retreating into
contemplation for years at a time. This absence was different: no prayer
reached her, no vision bore her mark, and no dream carried her voice.
For nine days and nine nights, she seemed to disappear from the world
entirely.

Even the gods could not find her. The incident unsettled them more
deeply than they admitted, for if one of the Nine could become lost,
what did that imply about the nature of reality itself?

On the tenth day she returned. The ancient chronicles describe her
emerging from a pool of still water beneath moonlight, though whether
this occurred literally or symbolically remains uncertain. What is known
is that she had changed. Never again would she speak of the future with
confidence.

When questioned by her fellow gods, she refused to explain fully what
she had witnessed; only fragments escaped her silence. She had seen
countless futures. In some the gods remained, civilization endured,
conflict diminished, knowledge accumulated, and the world grew safe -
and stagnant. Humanity ceased striving because there was nothing left to
strive against. Progress slowed, discovery faded, and while the world
survived, it ceased growing.

In other futures the gods departed. Humanity suffered greatly, kingdoms
collapsed, wars erupted, and entire civilizations vanished. And yet from
that suffering emerged wonders that even the Celestine had never
imagined: knowledge flourished, magic advanced, and humanity
accomplished impossible things.

Yet Selyra had seen something else - something she never fully
described, something waiting beyond the edge of creation, connected to
the Sea Beyond. When Orinth pressed her for answers, she reportedly
offered only a single warning: *"Not all doors remain closed because
they are locked."*

Thereafter she spoke little of her vision. Yet those who knew her best
noticed that fear had entered her eyes, and the Moonmother had never
before feared anything.

# Of the Ascensionists

No movement would shape the future of Elonia more profoundly than the
Ascensionists. History often remembers them unfairly, as the crimes
committed by their successors have obscured the nobility of their
beginnings. For in their earliest days, the Ascensionists possessed no
desire to rival the gods. They sought only preparedness.

Their founding question was simple: *What would happen if the gods
disappeared?*

The question scandalized many, but it fascinated Orinth. The god
personally attended several of their early gatherings, disguising
himself as an elderly scholar and engaging in arguments that lasted for
days.

The Ascensionists rejected complacency. They believed civilization had
become dangerously dependent upon miracle; if the gods truly wished
humanity to thrive, then humanity must eventually learn to solve its own
problems.

The movement spread rapidly. New magical techniques appeared every
decade, healers developed methods independent of divine blessing,
engineers constructed systems that reduced reliance on sacred
intervention, communication networks connected distant cities, and
artificial servants carried out labor once dependent upon magic.

For a time, the movement represented the best qualities of humanity:
creativity, determination, curiosity, and a willingness to confront
limitations. Even Myrraen regarded them with cautious admiration.
Unknowingly, she was looking upon the first people who truly embodied
her philosophy. They believed humanity should stand on its own.

The tragedy lay in where that belief eventually led.

# Of the Ascension Engine

There exists a point in every tragedy beyond which events become
unavoidable. For Elonia, that moment was the creation of the Ascension
Engine.

The origins of the device were innocent enough. The Ascensionists had
conquered disease, alleviated famine, and advanced magical knowledge
beyond anything previously imagined. Yet one obstacle remained: death.
No invention could defeat it, no theorem could solve it, and no spell
could permanently overcome it. Mortality remained beyond their reach.

And so they asked a new question: *If the gods could return the dead to
life, why could mortals not learn the same secret?*

At first the project received widespread support, and even some among
the gods viewed it favourably. The stated goal was noble - no more
grieving parents, no more wasted lives, no more final farewells. The
engineers of the project sincerely believed they were creating a
paradise.

Early results appeared miraculous. Fragments of memory could be
preserved after death, residual essence could be measured, and rarely,
fleeting communication with recently deceased individuals became
possible. Applause followed, funding increased, and expectations grew.

Then came the failures: a soul restored incompletely, a mind returned
fractured, a consciousness trapped within magical machinery. The
accidents multiplied, yet each disaster produced a familiar response:
the next attempt would succeed, the breakthrough was close, further
sacrifices were necessary.

Thus began the descent. Entire populations vanished into increasingly
dangerous experiments. Thousands died, then tens of thousands, and still
the project continued. For the architects genuinely believed they stood
upon the threshold of salvation. The horror of the Ascension Engine was
not malice; it was conviction.

The first god to understand the danger was Varek, and what he discovered
terrified him. The Engine was not merely trapping souls - it was
damaging the pathways through which souls traveled. The boundary between
life and death had begun to fracture. Something fundamental was being
wounded, something older than the gods themselves.

Thereafter Varek demanded the project's immediate destruction: no
compromise, no delay, no further experimentation. But by then the
machine had become more than a machine. It had become an idea, and ideas
are far harder to destroy.

# Of the Fracturing of Heaven

Never before had the Nine stood so divided. The Ascension Engine
transformed philosophical disagreement into outright conflict.

Aurelion argued that the project must be dismantled by force, as its
creators had proven themselves incapable of restraint. Varek agreed:
every day the Engine continued operating increased the damage. Iskara
mourned the suffering it caused, yet she hesitated to condemn those
responsible completely, for many acted from hope rather than cruelty.

Thaeron found himself torn between principles. Humanity possessed the
right to pursue greatness, yet greatness purchased through atrocity was
no greatness at all. Kaelis defended freedom, even the freedom to make
terrible mistakes. Orinth maintained that humanity must choose its own
path, for without that freedom, every achievement became meaningless.
Increasingly, however, his certainty faltered, as he too had witnessed
the horrors unfolding around the Engine.

And in the midst of these debates stood Myrraen: watching, listening,
and beginning, perhaps, to lose faith. Not in humanity, but in herself.

For she had spent ages alleviating suffering wherever she found it. Now
she wondered whether those countless acts of mercy had weakened the very
people she hoped to help. The question became inescapable: *If
civilization collapsed whenever divine support was withdrawn, had the
gods truly nurtured humanity, or merely sheltered it?*

No answer satisfied her.

# Of the Last Council

The final gathering of the Celestine occurred within the Hall of Dawn -
the same place where the First Covenant had been sworn. The symbolism
was not lost upon them.

The council lasted thirteen days, and its discussions shaped history. No
complete record survives; only scattered testimonies and fragments
preserved by priests, scholars, and later traditions remain. From these
fragments a broad picture emerges.

Aurelion argued that humanity required stronger guidance: the dangers
revealed by the Ascension Engine could no longer be ignored, and if
necessary, the gods must assume direct authority. Kaelis condemned the
proposal, viewing it as a betrayal of everything the Covenant stood for.
Orinth defended human choice, Selyra described futures drenched in
sorrow, Varek warned that reality itself had begun to fray, Thaeron
questioned whether strength could exist without struggle, and Iskara
pleaded for compassion toward both victims and perpetrators alike.

For nearly two weeks the debate continued, yet no agreement emerged. At
last all attention turned toward Myrraen, who for centuries had remained
unusually silent.

And now she rose. The surviving traditions disagree regarding her exact
speech, yet one line appears in almost every account:

*"If our children cannot stand without us, what have we actually taught
them?"*

Silence followed. No answer came; perhaps there was none. And in that
silence, a decision was made.

# The Silence

The final departure of the gods cannot be described with certainty, for
too many accounts contradict one another. Some say the sky darkened;
others speak of impossible light. Some claim every bell in the world
rang simultaneously; others insist all sound vanished.

Only one fact remains uncontested: the gods left.

The connection between Elonia and the divine was severed. Prayers ceased
receiving answers, miracles disappeared, and the great temples fell
silent. The Age of Revelation ended.

Yet not all the Nine departed. One remained. Orinth, lover of questions,
could not abandon the world entirely. How he remained hidden, none now
know; only that he did - watching, waiting, remembering.

Thus ended the age in which gods and mortals walked together. The roads
endured, the cities endured, and the rivers endured, yet everything had
changed. For the world had lost the certainty of heaven, and humanity,
for the first time, stood alone beneath the stars.

The long night had begun, and history entered the age that would one day
be known as the World Without Gods.

Book III: The World Without Gods

# The Years of Ash

No catastrophe in history has ever equalled the Silence. Generations had
lived believing that the gods were woven into the fabric of existence
itself, and few truly comprehended what their departure would mean until
it had already happened.

The first years were marked less by violence than disbelief. Priests
continued conducting rituals that no longer produced miracles; pilgrims
traveled to sacred sites expecting signs that never came; and entire
cities gathered in temples waiting for answers that would never arrive.
Again and again, prayers vanished into silence. For the first time in
recorded history, humanity encountered uncertainty without divine
reassurance.

Across Elonia, panic spread - not because armies marched or monsters
attacked, but because certainty had died.

The collapse unfolded slowly at first, then all at once. Regions that
relied upon sacred healing suffered devastating epidemics, agricultural
systems dependent upon divine blessing failed, and political alliances
established through the authority of the Celestine broke apart. The
institutions of the Age of Revelation suddenly discovered that they had
been built upon foundations now absent.

Many survived. Many did not. The Nine Cities endured the initial shock,
but most would not survive what followed.

# The Fall of the Nine Cities

No age truly ends until its symbols perish. For the Age of Revelation,
those symbols were the Nine Cities.

The first to fall was Nythara. For centuries its prophets had guided
statesmen and rulers through visions granted by Selyra. When those
visions ceased, confusion spread rapidly: predictions failed, ancient
certainties shattered, and internal divisions emerged. Within a century
the city fragmented, and by the time neighboring kingdoms arrived, its
great observatories stood abandoned, their pools reflecting only
ordinary moonlight.

Of Vale Myrr there remains only sorrow. The sanctuary endured longer
than most cities, its people continuing to believe in Myrraen's
teachings - perhaps longer than she herself would have expected. Yet
mercy proved difficult to sustain in a world increasingly shaped by
scarcity. Refugees arrived faster than the city could support them,
resources dwindled, and political tensions escalated.

When war finally reached its borders, the city discovered that goodwill
alone could not stop armies. The walls it had never needed suddenly
mattered, and there were none. Vale Myrr burned. To this day, its
destruction is regarded as one of the great tragedies of Elonian
history - not simply because lives were lost, but because an idea died:
the idea that all people could be welcomed, and that compassion alone
could protect a civilization. Many later philosophers blamed the
destruction of Vale Myrr for hardening the moral character of the ages
that followed. Humanity had looked upon pure mercy, and watched it fail.

Ilyanor endured longest. The City of Knowledge adapted more successfully
than any other remnant of the divine age. When miracles vanished, its
scholars turned to study; when sacred power disappeared, they turned to
arcane power; and when certainty died, they turned to questions. The
city's universities became the foundation of a new civilization, one no
longer dependent upon gods. Many historians would later argue that this
was Orinth's greatest legacy: not knowledge, but resilience. He had
taught humanity how to think, and when the gods disappeared, that lesson
remained.

# The Age of Ashes

The three centuries following the Silence became known collectively as
the Age of Ashes. The name is misleading, as people hear it and imagine
endless ruin, but the reality was far more complex.

There was suffering - more than the world had known for thousands of
years. Kingdoms collapsed, famines spread, wars erupted, and millions
died. Yet there was also invention, adaptation, and determination. The
old world crumbled, and a new one emerged from its remains.

Humanity learned to survive not because the gods returned, but because
they did not. Every challenge previously solved through miracle now
demanded mortal solutions: medicine advanced, agriculture improved, and
arcane research accelerated dramatically. In some regions, technological
progress during the Age of Ashes exceeded anything achieved during the
previous millennium.

Necessity became the engine of civilization - exactly as Myrraen had
predicted, and far more painfully than she had imagined.

# The Hidden God

Unknown to nearly everyone, one member of the Celestine remained:
Orinth.

The reasons remain debated. Some traditions claim he lacked the strength
to cross the Veil, while others insist he refused. The Church of the
Last Light preserves a different account: according to their oldest
texts, Orinth stayed because he could not answer a question. If the
purpose of the Abandonment was to allow humanity to choose its own
future, who would witness that choice?

And so he remained - not as a ruler or savior, but as an observer, a
teacher, and a keeper of memory.

His power diminished enormously. The god who had once guided
civilizations now struggled to perform even minor miracles. This
weakness became his protection, as the surviving Celestine beyond the
Veil perceived only the faintest trace of his presence. Humanity forgot
him, history forgot him, but he remained - watching, waiting, learning,
even now.

# Of the Missing Dead

For nearly seven centuries after the Silence, the dead continued their
journey. Funeral rites worked, necromancers received expected responses,
and spiritual traditions remained stable.

Then subtle irregularities appeared. At first only Varek's oldest
priesthood noticed: certain souls failed to arrive. Not many - not
enough to provoke panic - but enough to raise questions. Over time the
disappearances increased, ghosts became more common, ancient places
acquired strange echoes, and spiritual phenomena once considered
impossible emerged throughout the world.

The problem grew slowly, century by century, like a wound widening.
Nobody understood the cause, as the truth had been buried alongside the
Ascension Engine: the damage inflicted during the final years before the
Silence had never truly healed. And somewhere beyond the mortal world,
something had begun collecting what was lost.

# The Myth of Return

One thousand years after the departure of the gods, most people no
longer viewed the Celestine as historical figures. They were myths,
stories, symbols, and religious ideas. The Silence had become ancient
history, and many doubted it had ever happened at all.

This ignorance carried consequences. The memory of what the gods truly
were began disappearing; their virtues remained, but their flaws did
not. Humanity remembered benevolent saviors and forgot complicated
beings - it forgot arguments, disagreements, mistakes, and fear.

The gods became idealized, and because they became idealized, a
dangerous question emerged: *If the gods were so wonderful, why not
bring them back?*

# The First Ascendants

The movement that would eventually threaten the world began quietly. Its
founders were not villains; many were scholars, historians, priests, and
political reformers - people who looked upon the fractured modern world
and saw endless evidence of failure: war, corruption, poverty, and
division.

They believed humanity's great experiment had run its course. Myrraen
had been wrong: the gods were necessary, and humanity needed guidance
once again.

And so they began searching ancient ruins for forgotten knowledge. What
they found would change everything.

# The Last Light

Far from political power, hidden within a forgotten chapel, another
story continued - a story most of the world had forgotten, older than
nations.

An elderly priest named Cael tended a small congregation. They possessed
no armies, no influence, and no wealth - only a lantern marked with a
star, and a secret: the gods had not died. One remained.

And for many years, Orinth had been asking the same question that
haunted the world itself: not whether the gods *should* return, but
whether they *ought* to.

# Epilogue

And thus ends the Third History.

From the first awakening of the Nine to the long centuries after their
departure, the history of Elonia is not merely the history of gods or
kings; it is the history of dependence and freedom, of love and
consequence, of faith and uncertainty.

Myrraen sought to grant humanity independence; Aurelion feared the cost;
Orinth believed the choice belonged to humanity itself. A thousand years
later, the question remains unanswered. For Elonia survived, yet
survival alone is not proof. The gods departed, the world endured, and
still the final judgment has not been rendered.

Perhaps it never will be. For the story of Elonia did not end with the
Silence - it merely became ours.

#  Appendix I: The Nine Celestine

### Myrraen, The Last Mercy

Of all the Celestine, none are more misunderstood by later history.
Modern memory often reduces Myrraen to a single act: the Abandonment.
Yet for thousands of years before that event, she was perhaps the most
beloved being in the world.

Myrraen embodied mercy in its broadest sense - not merely forgiveness,
but compassion, protection, conscience, and the belief that people could
become better than they were. Unlike Aurelion, who sought justice
through law, Myrraen sought justice through understanding. Unlike
Thaeron, who believed hardship forged strength, she instinctively sought
to relieve suffering wherever she found it.

Her central flaw was the same quality that made her admirable: she cared
too deeply. The Abandonment was not born from hatred of humanity, but
from fear that divine protection had become a cage. Her tragedy lies in
the possibility that she may have been right.

### Aurelion, The Sovereign Flame

Aurelion governed law, civilization, authority, order, and ambition.
Many modern depictions portray him as stern, but ancient writings reveal
a more nuanced figure. Aurelion genuinely believed civilization was
humanity's greatest achievement, viewing law as a tool that allowed
countless individuals to coexist peacefully despite conflicting desires.

His opposition to Myrraen arose because he viewed abandonment as
irresponsibility; to Aurelion, power created obligation. A parent does
not stop guiding a child simply because the child resents guidance. His
tragedy is that history may have vindicated many of his fears.

### Selyra, The Moonmother

Selyra remains the most enigmatic member of the Celestine. Governing
prophecy, dreams, intuition, secrets, and the unseen foundations of
reality, she rarely communicated directly like the other gods,
preferring symbols, visions, and possibilities.

Her gift of foresight isolated her from both gods and mortals, as she
experienced history not as a sequence of events, but as countless
branching futures. It is widely believed that Selyra foresaw both the
Abandonment and its consequences long before either occurred; whether
she failed to prevent them or allowed them to happen remains unknown.

### Thaeron, The Iron Heart

Thaeron was never a god of conquest; he was a god of courage, a
distinction that mattered deeply to him. He admired warriors who fought
to defend others and despised those who sought glory through violence.
Ancient knightly traditions, military codes, and honor cultures all
trace their origins to his teachings.

Of all the gods except perhaps Varek, Thaeron understood the necessity
of hardship most clearly. Strength, in his view, existed only because
weakness existed.

### Elyndra, The Verdant Queen

Elyndra represented life in all its forms. Unlike many of the other
Celestine, she cared little for nations, ideologies, or political
structures, fixing her attention upon ecosystems rather than kingdoms.
To Elyndra, humanity was part of nature, not separate from it.

Many ancient conflicts arose because she opposed attempts to dominate or
reshape natural systems for purely political gain. Her worship survived
more successfully than most after the Abandonment because nature itself
remained.

### Varek, Keeper of the Final Door

No god was more feared, yet no god was less cruel. Varek taught that
endings are necessary; death, memory, change, and loss all fell under
his domain. In his philosophy, a life gains meaning precisely because it
cannot last forever.

His greatest responsibility was maintaining the passage of souls.
Something damaged that process during the final centuries before the
Abandonment, and the fact that souls now disappear suggests Varek's
ancient fears may have been justified.

### Orinth, The Laughing Sage

Orinth loved questions more than answers. Governing knowledge,
discovery, invention, language, creativity, and learning, he encouraged
disagreement, experimentation, and intellectual freedom. Among the gods,
he understood humanity most deeply because he genuinely enjoyed being
surprised.

Unlike Aurelion, he did not seek order; unlike Myrraen, he did not seek
moral improvement. He sought possibility. His decision to remain behind
after the Abandonment may be the single most important act in modern
Elonian history.

### Iskara, The Red Heart

Iskara embodied love in every form - romantic love, familial love,
friendship, loyalty, and devotion. Her philosophy centered upon
individual relationships rather than institutions. Where others judged
humanity according to empires and centuries, Iskara judged it according
to acts of sacrifice between ordinary people.

Her disagreements with Myrraen emerged because she believed humanity's
capacity for love outweighed its failures.

### Kaelis, The Wandering Star

Kaelis represented freedom, exploration, fortune, rebellion, and change.
He was beloved by sailors, travelers, adventurers, revolutionaries, and
anyone dissatisfied with the place they had been assigned.

To Kaelis, uncertainty was not a danger; it was the source of meaning.
He feared stagnation above all things and often found himself opposing
Aurelion's desire for stability. Many later philosophers traced the
ideals of personal liberty directly to his teachings.

## Appendix II: The Chronology of Elonia

# The Mythic Ages

The earliest periods of history cannot be reliably dated. Most records
from these ages originate from divine traditions, surviving oral
histories, and fragmentary temple archives. Modern historians disagree
about many details; nevertheless, the broad sequence of events is widely
accepted.

# Before Time

#### The Sea Beyond

Before any recorded history, before the appearance of gods or mortals,
there existed the metaphysical reality known as the Sea Beyond. Whether
the Sea Beyond is a place, a state of existence, or something else
entirely remains unknown. It is from this primordial mystery that the
Nine are believed to have emerged.

### The Age of Dawn

*c. Unknown Duration*

- **The Awakening:** The Nine Celestine awaken. Orinth becomes the first
  known god to gain self-awareness; Selyra dreams the stars; Varek
  introduces mortality to the world.

- **The Divine Assembly:** The Nine discover one another in the unformed
  world.

- **The Great Shaping:** The shaping of Elonia begins. Mountains,
  rivers, forests, and complex ecosystems are cultivated.

- **The Elder Era:** Dragons, giants, and other elder peoples flourish
  across the lands.

- **The Pre-Human World:** No evidence of humanity yet exists. This
  period represents the earliest known era of physical existence.

### The Age of Wonder

*c. Unknown Duration*

- **The Unforeseen Event:** Humanity first appears, though the exact
  origins of mortals remain unexplained in divine or mortal texts.

- **First Contact:** The Celestine discover human civilization and
  become fascinated by humanity's unpredictability and capacity for
  change.

- **The Early Mentors:** Orinth becomes humanity's first teacher, while
  Selyra perceives humanity's unusual and fracturing relationship with
  fate.

- **The Spark of Mercy:** Myrraen begins her lifelong devotion to
  mortals, observing their capacity for suffering and self-sacrifice.

- **The Dawn of History:** The appearance of humanity marks the true
  beginning of recorded history.

# The Divine Age

#### The First Covenant

*c. 3,500 BS (Before the Silence)*

- **The Divine Epiphany:** The Nine reveal themselves openly without
  disguise or mystery.

- **The Valley of Serath:** Human rulers gather in the Valley of Serath
  before the assembled Celestine.

- **The Oath of Partnership:** The First Covenant is sworn, establishing
  partnership rather than ownership or subjugation.

- **The Age Begins:** Humanity formally enters a grand era of
  co-creation with the gods, marking the traditional beginning of the
  Divine Age and the Age of Revelation.

#### The Early Revelation

*3,500 – 3,000 BS*

- **Rise of Kingdoms:** The first major mortal kingdoms emerge across
  the continent.

- **Spread of Faith:** Divine temples and sanctuaries spread across
  Elonia, serving as centers of learning and governance.

- **Illumination:** Large-scale literacy increases dramatically under
  Orinth's guidance.

- **Arcane Roots:** The earliest organized magical traditions appear,
  blending mortal intellect with divine principle.

- **Global Connections:** Intercontinental trade routes develop, linking
  distant settlements.

#### The Founding of the Nine Cities

*3,100 – 2,800 BS*

- **Solcaris:** Raised upon sunlit plains beside the Sea of Glass,
  becoming the sanctuary of law and governance under Aurelion.

- **Ilyanor:** Established as the absolute center of learning and
  philosophy under Orinth.

- **Nythara:** Built among silver mountains, becoming the foremost city
  of prophecy and cosmic observation under Selyra.

- **Vale Myrr:** Established as an unwalled sanctuary of mercy and
  refuge under Myrraen.

- **Harad Veyr:** Developed in the northern lands as a city of
  discipline and defensive courage under Thaeron.

- **The Complete Circle:** Additional great cities emerge, becoming the
  cultural, spiritual, and political hearts of civilization.

#### The Great Concord

*2,800 – 1,600 BS* *Often regarded as the greatest era in Elonian
history.*

- **Universal Peace:** Widespread prosperity and a massive reduction in
  inter-state warfare.

- **Intellectual Flowering:** Rapid expansion of magical scholarship and
  philosophical inquiry.

- **Golden Infrastructure:** International cooperation leads to the
  construction of the Golden Paths and monumental architecture.

- **Mortal Flourishing:** Extraordinary population growth and
  agricultural abundance.

- **Peak Synergy:** The influence of the Celestine reaches its absolute
  height; later generations romanticize this period as humanity's true
  golden age.

#### The Rise of Avarr

*c. 1,900 BS*

- **The Imperial Jewel:** Avarr emerges as the dominant civilization of
  the age, situated at the crossroads of continental trade.

- **Cultural Hegemony:** It becomes the world's greatest political,
  economic, and intellectual power, its institutions copied by
  neighboring states.

- **The Crown of the Covenant:** For centuries, Avarr appears to embody
  the complete success of the First Covenant and mortal stewardship.

#### The Atrocities of Avarr

*c. 1,600 BS*

- **The Hubris of Certainty:** Reasoning itself into moral superiority,
  Avarr launches systematic cultural eradication campaigns against
  neighboring peoples.

- **The Descent:** Entire societies are forcibly assimilated, their
  languages forbidden, traditions erased, and communities displaced.

- **Divine Intervention:** The Celestine intervene to stop the
  atrocities; the corrupt leadership is removed and the victims are
  protected.

- **The Shadow:** Though the empire survives, it never fully recovers
  its moral standing. The event profoundly affects Myrraen, leading many
  historians to identify this as the moment the seed of the Abandonment
  was planted.

# The Waning Age

#### The Century of Interventions

*1,500 – 1,300 BS*

- **Over-Reliance:** Divine involvement in mortal affairs reaches
  unprecedented levels; miracles become expected routine solutions for
  famine, disease, and conflict.

- **The Atrophy of Will:** Reliance upon divine assistance weakens
  mortal self-reliance and caution.

- **Heavenly Debate:** Philosophical disagreements increase among the
  Celestine regarding the ethics of constant intervention.

- **The Growing Question:** Myrraen begins questioning whether shielding
  humanity from every consequence has become fundamentally harmful.

#### The Dream of Selyra

*c. 1,250 BS*

- **The Nine-Day Absence:** Selyra vanishes entirely from the world and
  the divine sphere, unreachable by prayer or vision.

- **The Changed Goddess:** She returns deeply altered, refusing to speak
  of the future with certainty.

- **The Fractured Visions:** Reports emerge of disturbing prophecies
  detailing futures of divine stagnation or catastrophic mortal
  suffering.

- **The Sea Beyond:** References to an unknown, ancient force waiting
  beyond the Sea Beyond first appear, sparking quiet dread among the
  Nine.

#### The Rise of the Ascensionists

*1,200 – 1,050 BS*

- **Mortal Self-Reliance:** Intellectual movements advocating complete
  mortal self-sufficiency spread rapidly through universities.

- **Arcane Revolution:** Arcane sciences flourish, leading to non-divine
  advances in medicine, engineering, and labor.

- **Divine Patronage:** Orinth strongly supports these developments as a
  triumph of curiosity; Myrraen quietly admires their desire to stand
  alone.

#### Construction of the Ascension Engine

*c. 1,000 BS*

- **Conquering Death:** Driven by a desire to defeat mortality, scholars
  and engineers begin research into capturing and restoring human souls.

- **The Great Project:** Backed by powerful political and academic
  institutions, the Ascension Engine becomes the largest and most
  ambitious scientific undertaking in history.

#### The Soul Crisis

*980 – 950 BS*

- **Catastrophic Failures:** Experimental failures multiply, leaving
  souls trapped, fractured, or destroyed within magical machinery.

- **Mass Casualties:** Tens of thousands vanish into increasingly
  desperate experiments in the name of salvation.

- **Cosmic Damage:** Varek discovers that the Engine has severely
  damaged the fundamental pathways of death and soul passage.

- **Moral Fracture:** Deep philosophical and political divisions tear
  mortal society apart, marking the first genuine existential crisis of
  the Divine Age.

#### The Fracturing of Heaven

*c. 930 BS*

- **Open Conflict:** Philosophical disagreement among the Celestine
  turns into outright division.

- **The Positions:** Aurelion demands direct divine authority over
  mortals; Kaelis defends mortal freedom to err; Orinth protects human
  choice; Varek demands the total destruction of the Engine.

- **The Broken Unity:** Myrraen loses confidence in the Covenant, and
  the ancient unity of the Nine effectively ends.

#### The Last Council

*901 BS*

- **The Thirteen Days:** The Nine gather for the final time at the Hall
  of Dawn in Serath.

- **The Deadlock:** Debates rage without consensus over whether to rule,
  assist, or abandon.

- **The Defining Question:** Myrraen rises and poses the question that
  seals history: *"If our children cannot stand without us, what have we
  actually taught them?"*

# The Silence

#### The Abandonment

*900 BS / Year 0 AS (After the Silence)*

- **The Severing:** Myrraen severs the connection between Elonia and the
  divine, leading the Celestine beyond the Veil.

- **The Silence:** Miracles cease instantly, sacred power vanishes, and
  prayers go unanswered.

- **The Age Ends:** The Age of Revelation comes to an abrupt end.

- **The Secret Keeper:** Orinth secretly remains behind as a hidden
  observer, unable to abandon humanity. Year 900 BS becomes Year 0 AS.

# The World Without Gods

#### The Years of Ash

*0 – 200 AS*

- **The Great Shock:** Widespread societal collapse driven by disbelief,
  panic, and institutional failure.

- **Institutional Decay:** Religious organizations fragment, divine
  medical systems fail, and regional famines spread.

- **The Dark Decades:** Political alliances collapse without divine
  mediation, leading to the most difficult transition period in recorded
  history.

#### The Fall of the Nine Cities

*20 – 350 AS*

- **Nythara (c. 100 AS):** Deprived of prophetic visions, internal
  divisions fracture the city; its observatories are abandoned.

- **Vale Myrr (c. 180 AS):** The unwalled sanctuary of mercy is overrun
  by desperate refugees and armies competing for resources; the city
  burns.

- **Solcaris & Harad Veyr:** Solcaris fragments as written laws lose
  divine enforcement; Harad Veyr gradually declines into regional
  warlordism.

- **Ilyanor:** The City of Knowledge adapts successfully, replacing
  divine miracles with arcane science and remaining as the lone
  surviving beacon.

#### The Age of Ashes

*0 – 300 AS*

- **Mortal Adaptation:** Humanity learns to survive entirely on its own
  terms out of sheer necessity.

- **Scientific Acceleration:** Arcane scholarship, engineering, and
  empirical medicine advance at a rate exceeding the prior millennium.

- **New Foundations:** Modern political and economic institutions emerge
  from the ruins of the divine age.

#### The Hidden Centuries

*300 – 700 AS*

- **The Hidden God:** Orinth wanders Elonia in secret, his power greatly
  diminished.

- **The Last Light:** The secretive Church of the Last Light forms to
  preserve the truth of the remaining god and historical memory.

- **Mythologization:** True history fades into legend; the Celestine are
  increasingly viewed as mythological or idealized figures rather than
  real, complex entities.

#### The Missing Dead

*c. 650 AS*

- **The Unhealed Wound:** Ancient damage inflicted by the Ascension
  Engine manifests fully. Souls begin disappearing before reaching their
  final destination.

- **Spiritual Instability:** Ghost sightings, spiritual anomalies, and
  untethered necrotic phenomena increase dramatically worldwide, proving
  Varek's ancient warnings correct.

#### The Age of Nations

*700 – 1,050 AS*

- **Modern Order:** Stable, fully mortal kingdoms and republics
  establish new continental trade networks.

- **Institutional Power:** Arcane universities and secular academies
  become the true centers of power and innovation.

- **High Arcana:** Arcane magic achieves unprecedented sophistication,
  fulfilling the Ascensionist ideal of mortal self-reliance.

#### The First Ascendants

*c. 1,020 AS*

- **Rediscovery:** Scholars uncover buried pre-Silence texts detailing
  the true power of the divine age.

- **The Movement:** Disillusioned with mortal corruption and war, secret
  circles advocating the forced return of the Celestine form across
  major cities.

#### Present Day

*c. 1,100 AS*

- **The Threshold:** The Ascendants actively seek methods to breach the
  Veil and recall the gods.

- **The Keeper's Vigil:** The Church of the Last Light works quietly to
  protect Orinth's secret and prevent the return of the pantheon.

- **Cosmic Crisis:** The disappearance of souls accelerates, ancient
  ruins awaken, and the Veil thins.

- **The Imminent Turning Point:** Exactly one thousand years after the
  Abandonment, the balance established by Myrraen's sacrifice begins to
  fail, placing Elonia on the brink of its next great age.
